Reflections on the Road Back Home

Session 19 of 22 5 min read Return Journey

Sad. Tired. And reluctant to get up. It wasn’t because of fatigue; it was because we did not want to leave. But as the saying goes, every good thing must come to an end.

Asia, Europe, America, and Africa—I’ve seen them all,
But nowhere is there a place like Qadian.

The admins, Harris and Nabeel bhai, were busy getting everyone ready, but even they seemed weighed down by the sadness of departure.

Some of us may have risen early for one final prayer at Darul Masih. Perhaps a few quietly slipped out to Bahishti Maqbara for one last visit. I wouldn’t know, being a chaperone kept me occupied—ensuring not only that I packed everything but also helping others double-check their belongings.

The buses were loaded, and we said our final goodbyes. Standing together for one last congregational silent prayer, we asked Allah for His guidance and protection before we boarded the buses and left Qadian.

Our minds were already racing ahead to prepare for our journey back—Delhi Mosque, Delhi Airport, and eventually home. But there was no denying it: we had left our hearts behind in Qadian.

Once we roamed its streets,
Now the streets of Qadian roam before our eyes.

Reflections on the Journey

On the bus, one of our young Waqf-e-Nau members took on the role of MC, keeping the passengers entertained by asking general knowledge and Jamaat-related questions to teams. While some of us participated, others nodded off into naps—it was an eight-hour drive, after all.

For me, it was a mix of brief naps and deep reflection. What did this journey mean to me? What did it mean to visit these sacred sites in Delhi, Agra, and Qadian?

The Mughals ruled most of India for over 400 years, leaving behind architectural marvels like the Red Fort, Qutub Minar, and the Taj Mahal. But where are the Mughals now? Their marvels and their forts stand as relics of a bygone era. Ahmadiyyat, on the other hand, established in 1889, stands as an eternal legacy. And by the grace of Allah, it will endure forever.

We are here in India to refresh our faith in Islam and Ahmadiyyat, to see firsthand the legacy of the Promised Messiah (as), and to remind ourselves of the blessings we are part of.

People from all over the world visit various tombs, seeking history or grandeur. But it is the Promised Messiah’s grave in Bahishti Maqbara—and the graves of his companions—where people sincerely offer prayers and send Salam.

The Mughals built the Red Fort in New Delhi to protect themselves from their enemies, but Allah revealed to Hazrat Promised Messiah (as):

I will save all who dwell in the House.

Was this revelation referring to the physical house of Darul Masih, to Qadian, or to Ahmadiyyat as a whole? Regardless, we all felt that we were within the fort of Allah, with our commanders being the Holy Prophet Muhammad (sa) and Hazrat Promised Messiah (as). No enemy can ever conquer this fort.

The Mughals had their Diwan-e-Khas and Diwan-e-Aam, grand halls for private and public gatherings. But can they compare to the humble rooms of Hazrat Amma Jaan or Hazrat Promised Messiah? What about the gatherings atop Masjid Mubarak, where revelations descended, or the Shah Nasheen at Bahishti Maqbara, where companions sat in the presence of the Promised Messiah?

The light of Minaratul Masih shines far brighter in our hearts than the lights and sounds of the Red Fort’s nightly show. To us, Minaratul Masih holds a meaning so profound that Qutub Minar pales in comparison.

And what of the Taj Mahal, a celebrated symbol of love? It cannot rival the stories of Hazrat Promised Messiah’s love for his Creator.

The Promised Messiah (as) was mortal in the love of Allah. In his own words, this meant dissolving his soul in the divine, becoming one with Allah in his worship and devotion. His entire being became a masjid, a sanctuary for Allah’s presence.

He was nicknamed Maseetar, someone who lives in the mosque, and at some point, he seemed to embody the essence of a mosque itself. Hazrat Promised Messiah (as) built Masjid Mubarak even though Masjid Aqsa was just a short walk away. He created Baitul Riazat, Baitul Dua, and Baitul Fikr—spaces dedicated to worship, prayer, and reflection. Who else looks for so many opportunities to connect with their Creator?

His love for the Holy Prophet Muhammad (sa) was unparalleled, and I’ve written about it in my diary before, so I’ll skip it here. But have you heard the stories of the Promised Messiah’s love for Hazrat Amma Jaan? Or the stories of his beloved companions?

The Taj Mahal, with all its grandeur, has nothing on Qadian. I am glad we saw these historical landmarks in Delhi and Agra, for they gave us a chance to compare and realize the true significance of what we have in Qadian.

Qadian is better than the Red Fort, Qutub Minar, and even the Taj Mahal. It’s not just history; it’s living proof of Allah’s grace and the fulfillment of His promises.

Carrying the Light Forward

We are the group of Waqf-e-Nau. The children in our group are the future leaders of our Jamaat. On this journey, we have lit our candles from the eternal light of Hazrat Promised Messiah (as). Our duty now is clear: to take this light and illuminate the entire world.

O my dear friends, do not rest until
The flag of Qadian waves over the entire world.

May Allah enable us to fulfill our responsibilities and make this journey a source of lasting blessings. Ameen.
